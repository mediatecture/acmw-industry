# Core rules, myths and don'ts

## The five rules of a good industry resume

1. **Keep it short and front-loaded.** Length depends on stage: 1–2 pages for a fresh PhD
   graduate, 2 (up to 3) with a postdoc or other work, never more than 3. The top third of page one
   does most of the work, because that's often all a recruiter reads before deciding.
2. **A PhD is work experience.** List it under Experience as a role ("PhD Researcher, University
   of X, 2021–2025") with outcome bullets, not as a line under Education with a thesis title.
3. **Show metrics that matter.** Numbers make claims concrete and checkable: people, money, time,
   reach, selectivity, change. Choose the numbers this employer would care about, not the ones
   academia rewards (h-index, citation counts, impact factors).
4. **Curate and tailor.** Not everything you did belongs on every application. Keep a long base
   resume for yourself and cut a tailored version from it for each role.
5. **Online presence is part of it.** Someone will Google you. LinkedIn should tell the same story
   as the resume, a simple portfolio site helps (especially for design, UX and research roles),
   and the contact email should be professional and not a university address you'll lose.

The single principle underneath all five: **on a resume, don't tell them what you learned; show
them what you delivered.**

## Resume myths, and the answers

Use these when critiquing. Only raise the ones the resume actually falls for.

| Myth | Answer | Why |
|---|---|---|
| My resume can be longer than 3 pages | **No** | Nobody reads past page 2 closely. Length signals you can't prioritise. |
| My PhD counts as work experience | **Yes** | It's 3–5 years of self-directed, delivered, funded work. Present it as a job. |
| Managing a supervisor counts as stakeholder management | **Yes, if you show it** | Managing up, negotiating scope and timelines, and handling a panel or industry partners is real stakeholder work. "Stakeholder management" as a bare claim is worthless; a concrete example (e.g. "aligned 3 supervisors and an industry partner on scope") is not. |
| I should list my academic publications | **It depends** | See the publications rule below. |
| A recruiter or employer will read my entire resume | **No** | They skim. Front-load the strongest evidence. |
| Someone will Google me before an interview | **Yes** | Make sure what they find helps you. |
| I can use one resume for all job applications | **No** | Tailor every application from a base version. |
| I should mention my communication skills | **No** | Everyone claims it, so it says nothing. Show it instead: talks given, media coverage, workshops run, reports written for non-specialists. |
| My resume should lead with my education | **No** | Lead with a summary and experience. Education moves near the end. The "PhD" in your name line already signals it. |
| My email address can be party.pink.755@hotmail.com | **No** | Use firstname.lastname or similar, on a provider you'll keep. Not your uni email either: you'll lose it. |
| My h-index will impress an industry employer | **No** | Most won't know what it is, and those who do won't weigh it. Translate impact into reach, adoption or change. |

## The publications rule

- **Research-heavy roles** (industry R&D, research scientist, data science, policy research,
  research-intensive government or think-tank roles): list a **selected 3–5** publications, chosen
  for relevance to the role, under "Selected publications". Add the venue's acceptance rate or
  significance where it's impressive and not obvious.
- **All other roles**: no list. One line is enough, e.g. "Published 9 peer-reviewed papers; full
  list at [Google Scholar / portfolio link]". Better still, fold the outcome into an experience
  bullet ("published at international venues with 20–25% acceptance rates, with coverage on
  national TV and radio").

## Writing rules

- **Start bullets with action verbs**: led, built, secured, redesigned, delivered, ran, recruited,
  designed, launched, negotiated, analysed, taught.
- **Everything needs a number** (see "Digging for numbers" in SKILL.md).
- **Cut academic filler**: "was involved in" → "helped build"; "assisted with data collection" →
  "collected data"; "it could be argued that" → delete it.
- **Make it an exciting read.** Specific beats grand. "Recruited and interviewed 40 farmers across
  regional Victoria" is more interesting than "conducted extensive qualitative fieldwork".
- **Keep the full story for interviews.** A bullet is a hook, not the whole method section. If it
  needs a semicolon and three clauses to explain, it's too long.

## Words that give it away (AI or brochure language)

Hiring managers increasingly spot and discard AI-written applications. Flag and replace these, and
never introduce them in your own drafts:

> thrilled, excited to apply, passionate about, leverage, delve, journey, landscape, rapidly
> evolving, cutting-edge, innovative (as filler), dynamic, synergy, spearheaded (overused),
> drive meaningful impact, unique blend of, not just X but Y, transform(ing), robust, seamless,
> holistic, empower, foster, navigate (metaphorical), tapestry, testament to, in today's world

Also flag the patterns, not just the words: triplets of abstract nouns, "not just X, but Y"
constructions, em-dash-heavy sentences, and openers that praise the employer instead of saying
anything about the applicant.

A real example of what not to send:

> "I am thrilled to apply for this exciting opportunity at your innovative organisation. In today's
> rapidly evolving landscape, I am eager to leverage my passion for research and my unique blend of
> analytical and creative skills to drive meaningful impact. Throughout my PhD journey, I have
> delved deep into complex challenges — not just solving problems, but transforming them."

Every sentence there could be written by anyone about any job. It contains no fact about the person.

## Don'ts

- **Don't send genAI content.** Use tools to get feedback and structure; the words and facts must
  be yours.
- **Don't apply to 50 jobs in a day.** Ten tailored applications beat fifty generic ones.
- **Don't ignore selection criteria.** If the ad has them, they're how you get scored.
- **Don't use generic filenames** like `Resume_Final_v3.pdf` or `Canva_Export_20251001.pdf`. Use
  `Firstname_Lastname_Resume_Organisation.pdf`.
- **Don't apologise for being new** to industry. State what you've done.
- **Don't send generic cover letters.** If you send one, it should only make sense for this job.
- **Skip the photo and date of birth** (Australia and most English-speaking markets). Project
  images can work for design or creative roles; a headshot doesn't.
- **Don't use a fancy template.** Two-column Canva designs with skill bars and icons break
  applicant tracking systems and look like every other one. Plain, well-spaced, one column.
- **Don't send Word documents.** Send PDF, so it looks the way you made it.
- **Don't send what you don't love.** If you wouldn't be proud to have the hiring manager read
  every line, fix it before you send it.
