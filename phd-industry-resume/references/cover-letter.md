# Cover letters

A cover letter answers two questions for the hiring manager: **have they understood this job, and
what will they do for us?** It is not the resume retold in sentences, and it is not a statement of
how much the applicant wants the job.

The same drafting rules apply as for the resume (see SKILL.md): use only facts the user gave you,
leave `[?]` gaps for anything missing, and follow "How you write". This matters even more for a
cover letter, because it's the document where AI-written text is easiest to spot.

## What you need before drafting

1. **The job ad.** A letter can't be tailored without one. If they don't have one, say so, and
   offer a base letter with clearly marked gaps for the parts that must change per employer.
2. **Their resume** (or the facts from it), so you can pick the evidence.
3. **Why this organisation, in their words.** Ask: "What drew you to this organisation in
   particular? A project, a report, a product, a person you've spoken to, something they stand
   for?" Never invent this part. If they don't have an answer, suggest where to look (the
   organisation's recent reports, projects, news, people on LinkedIn, a coffee with someone who
   works there) and leave a `[why them?]` gap.
4. **Anything the ad asks for specifically**: a word limit, a "pitch", questions to answer, or a
   named contact person.

## The rules

- **One page.** Three or four short paragraphs, roughly 250–400 words. Academic job letters of two
  pages or more are a different genre; don't carry the habit over.
- **The only-this-job test.** If the letter would still make sense sent to a different employer,
  it isn't tailored. Every paragraph should contain at least one thing that's specific to this
  role or organisation.
- **Evidence, not adjectives.** "I'm a strong communicator" says nothing. "I've presented my
  research to 300 farmers at regional field days" says something.
- **What they get, not what you want.** Flexibility, salary, a supportive team and career growth
  matter, but they come up later, in interviews and negotiation.
- **No apology.** Don't open or close with a lack of industry experience. Say what you've done that
  fits the job.
- **No thesis summary.** One sentence about the PhD's topic at most, and only if it's relevant.
- **Address it to a person** where possible: the contact named in the ad (Australian ads often
  list one for enquiries), or the hiring manager on LinkedIn. Otherwise "Dear [team name] hiring
  team". Avoid "To Whom It May Concern" and "Dear Sir/Madam".
- **Same header as the resume** (name, email, phone, link), sent as a PDF named
  `Firstname_Lastname_CoverLetter_Organisation.pdf`.
- **Optional letters:** send a short tailored one if they can say something specific. If they
  can't, a generic letter does more harm than none.

### Government and university roles

Many Australian public sector and university roles ask for a "pitch" or statement (often 1–2 pages
or a word limit) instead of, or as well as, a cover letter, or for responses to selection
criteria. Follow the ad exactly: its word limit, its format, and its criteria. Where criteria are
listed, each one needs a specific example, ideally in STAR form. Don't fold criteria into a general
letter when the ad asks for them separately. See `australia.md`.

## Structure

**1. Opening (2–3 sentences).** Name the role and give the single strongest reason they fit,
specifically. Not "I am writing to apply for the position of…"; they know that from the subject
line.

**2. Evidence (1–2 paragraphs).** Two or three examples, each tied to one of the ad's top
priorities. For each: what they did, what came of it, and why it matters for this job. Don't
copy resume bullets; add the context a bullet can't hold.

**3. Why them (2–3 sentences).** Something true about this organisation that connects to their
own experience or interests. Not flattery ("your innovative organisation"). It has to come from
the user.

**4. Close (1–2 sentences).** Availability, work rights if relevant, and a plain thank you.
"Thank you for considering my application" is enough.

Then: "Kind regards," and their name.

## Worked example

Illustrative only: a fictional applicant and a fictional ad. Never reuse its facts for a user.

**The ad:** UX Researcher at a state health department's digital services team. Priorities: run
research with the public, including people who find digital services hard to use; turn findings
into design recommendations; work with designers and policy staff.

**Before (generic, AI-styled):**

> Dear Sir/Madam,
>
> I am writing to express my strong interest in the UX Researcher position at your innovative
> organisation. As a recent PhD graduate with a passion for human-centred design, I am thrilled at
> the opportunity to leverage my research skills to drive meaningful impact. My PhD journey has
> equipped me with excellent communication, analytical and teamwork skills. Although I do not have
> industry experience, I am a fast learner and I am confident I would be a great fit for your team.

What's wrong: no fact about the applicant, no fact about the job, an apology, and several words
from the list in `rules.md`. It passes neither the only-this-job test nor the any-human test.

**After (from the applicant's own facts):**

> Dear Digital Services hiring team,
>
> For the past four years I've studied how older adults use telehealth, which is why your UX
> Researcher role stood out: most of my work has been with people who find digital services hard
> to use.
>
> During my PhD I ran interviews and usability sessions with 45 people aged 70 and over, many in
> their homes and some with carers present. I turned the findings into design guidelines that a
> regional health service used to simplify its appointment booking pages. I also worked closely
> with the service's clinicians and IT team, so I'm used to explaining research to people who
> weren't in the room and agreeing on what to change.
>
> I read your team's recent report on making online forms accessible, and the section on carers
> completing forms on someone else's behalf matched what I saw in my fieldwork. I'd like to help
> build on that work.
>
> I'm available from February and have full working rights in Australia. Thank you for
> considering my application.
>
> Kind regards,
> [Name]

What changed: the opening says why this person suits this job; the evidence maps to the ad's
priorities (research with the public, hard-to-reach users, working with other disciplines); the
"why them" paragraph names something real and connects it to the applicant's experience; no
apology, no adjectives about themselves.

## Coaching flow

### Writing a new letter

1. Collect what's listed under "What you need before drafting". Ask for what's missing in one
   short message.
2. Pull the ad's 3–5 priorities (see `tailoring.md`, "Reading a job ad") and propose which two or
   three of their experiences to use as evidence. Let them confirm or swap.
3. Draft the letter from their facts, with `[?]` gaps for missing numbers and `[why them?]` if
   they haven't told you.
4. List the questions that would fill the gaps.
5. Tell them to read it aloud and change anything they wouldn't say. A cover letter should sound
   like a person.

### Critiquing a letter

1. **Assessment in two or three plain sentences**, pointing at specific sentences in their letter.
2. **The only-this-job test:** quote the sentences that could go to any employer.
3. **Check the opening line, the evidence, the "why them" paragraph and the length.**
4. **Flag** apologies, thesis summaries, statements of what they want, and any word from the list
   in `rules.md`.
5. **Rewrite** the weakest paragraph (or the whole letter if it needs it) from their facts, with
   `[?]` gaps.
